# Dust Colour Hysteresis in AGN

Repository name: `ir-echo-lab`.

A pre-registered test, on public WISE/NEOWISE photometry, of whether the mid-infrared colour of variable dust emission in active galactic nuclei (AGN) differs between brightening and fading at the same W2 flux. The pipeline is first checked on a known case (the 2020 flare of NGC 3786), then applied to two disjoint samples of hard-X-ray-selected AGN from the Swift-BAT 105-month catalogue. **Current status: the colour asymmetry is in the data. It survives a null test of the statistic, a direct comparison at matched W2 flux and a second photometry. Its cause is not determined: every process in which W1 responds before W2 gives the same sign. A fixed linear response in which W2 follows W1 with a median lag of 40 d and 50 d (observed frame, the two samples) predicts 95% and 99% of the median asymmetry, and no evidence was found for a component beyond it. That is consistent with an inter-band-lag model and does not separate it from delayed re-formation of dust: in synthetic tests re-formation alone, with no lag between the bands, is fitted as an apparent lag of 15–25 d. A later stage with the ZTF optical light curve as the driver (stage 10, type 1 objects only) gives the same sign, W2 later than W1, and optical-to-infrared lags shorter than the relation of Mandal et al. (2024); it does not separate a fixed response from re-formation either. The analysis stops at stage 10; candidates for later work are listed in [docs/FUTURE_WORK.md](docs/FUTURE_WORK.md).**

## Conclusions in three layers

**1. The colour asymmetry is in the data.** (Stage 8, an additional analysis whose criteria were committed before it was run; [docs/VALIDATION_8.md](docs/VALIDATION_8.md).)

- *Null test.* Synthetic light curves without a colour loop (W1 a linear function of W2, or one blackbody whose temperature alone changes) were given the real visit times, the real errors and the W1–W2 error correlation estimated per object (median r = 0.42 and 0.33 in the two samples), and passed through the same estimator, 200 times per case. The median D of the null samples is 0.000 (2.5–97.5%: −0.012 to +0.013 for `sample_v1`, −0.010 to +0.010 for `sample_v2`) and the fraction with D > 0 is 0.50. All 36 combinations (two samples, two null cases, nine variants of the estimator settings) meet the "unbiased" criterion. The observed medians, +0.228 and +0.268, are far above the 99th percentile of the null (+0.014, +0.012).
- *Direct comparison.* Without reference visits, excess fluxes, a shared peak temperature or a blackbody: within the range of W2 flux covered by both branches, W1 is brighter on the rising branch than on the fading branch in 180 of 211 objects (85%) in `sample_v1` and 194 of 200 (97%) in `sample_v2`. The median colour difference Δ(W1−W2), rising minus fading, is −17.5 mmag (95% interval −20.9 to −13.2) and −29.8 mmag (−33.4 to −25.8). On the loop-free null data the same estimator gives a positive fraction of 0.50 (0.42–0.57).
- *Second photometry.* With the unTimely catalogue the fractions are 170 of 218 (78%) and 173 of 192 (90%).
- A term for the scan direction changes the median by 0.0% and 1.7%. A linear time term, added after the plan was committed, leaves 179 of 211 and 189 of 200.

**2. The cause is not determined.** Any case in which W1 responds before W2 gives this sign: a lag between the bands from a fixed dust distribution, a W2 response that is broader, undelayed accretion-disc light in W1, or delayed re-formation of dust. The evidence for re-formation has not increased. The stage 6B verdict against the inter-band-lag model rested on an assumption: when the simulated driver follows the mass and luminosity scaling of MacLeod et al. (2010), the observed ρ = +0.129 lies outside the model (1.5–2.0% of mock samples at or below it) only if the lag–luminosity relation has no scatter; with 0.15 dex of scatter the fraction is 7.5–8.5% and with 0.30 dex it is 32%. The size of the real scatter was not found in the literature checked at that stage, so the lag model is no longer excluded. (Stage 9 found one value: Mandal et al. 2024, Table 5, give 0.18–0.19 dex for the lag–luminosity relation of each band, which lies between the two cases; it was read through a summarising tool, not from the PDF, and it is the scatter of each band's lag, not of their difference.) The mock samples do not reproduce the sample well either: they have 30–34 measurable objects where the data have 70, and a median D of 0.32–0.48 where the data have 0.27. The verdicts in the table below are left as they were recorded.

*Stage 9: does an inter-band lag alone give the size of the asymmetry?* (Models, criteria and the interpretation rule are in [docs/PREDICTIONS_9.md](docs/PREDICTIONS_9.md), committed before any of these models was fitted to a real light curve. The plan was written after the results up to stage 8 had been seen, and says so.)

- *Size.* For each object W2 was modelled as a + b·(K ∗ W1), with K a top-hat kernel of fixed lag and width, W1 interpolated linearly between visits and no extrapolation. The fitted lag of W2 behind W1 has a median of 40 d in `sample_v1` (225 objects) and 50 d in `sample_v2` (210 objects), in the observed frame, and is positive in 88% and 97% of the objects. The branch offset that this pure shift predicts, measured on the same visits with the stage 8 estimator, has a median of −15.7 and −26.8 mmag against a measured −16.5 and −27.2 mmag (197 and 185 objects): 95% and 99%. The median residual is −1.9 and −0.9 mmag. (The measured medians differ from the −17.5 and −29.8 mmag of layer 1 because the first and last visits cannot be predicted without extrapolation and are left out.)
- *No evidence for a component beyond a fixed kernel.* After the lag, neither a kernel width nor a term that offsets the two branches improves the fit beyond what synthetic samples built from pure shifts give. By the committed rule the four cells (two samples, pure shift and shift with a width) read "explained" twice and "undetermined" twice, and none reads "a part remains"; the overall label by that rule is "depends on the model", not "explained". In one undetermined cell the residual is closer to zero than one of the two null ranges and the fraction of objects with a bluer residual is below both; in the other that fraction is 0.02 above one of the two null ranges.
- *What this cannot tell apart.* Synthetic light curves with the real visit times and errors show that a lag of 40 or 80 d is recovered within 10 d at the sample level and that "no lag" is separated from "lag" with power 1.0. A pure shift is not separated from a kernel of different width or tail (power 0 in three of four configurations), so the width is not reported as a measurement. A model with delayed re-formation only, and no lag between the bands, is fitted as an apparent lag of 15–25 d and is absorbed by the fixed kernel (power 0). Undelayed accretion-disc light in W1 also leaves a fixed linear relation between the bands. The statement is therefore limited to: **consistent with an inter-band-lag model; not distinguished from re-formation.**
- *Lag against luminosity.* For the 347 objects with a bolometric luminosity the rest-frame lags (median 43 d) are 1.22 times the difference between the W2 and W1 lags of Lyu, Rieke & Smith (2019, Eqs. 15–16) in the median. Synthetic samples built with that relation return 0.79–0.96 through the same fit, so after that calibration the lags are about 1.4 times longer (1.36–1.44). The dependence on luminosity is weak: Spearman ρ = +0.38, against 0.60–0.76 in those synthetic samples. Label by the committed rule: "differs from the literature relation". The published relations differ among themselves: the W2 − W1 difference from Mandal et al. (2024, Table 5) is 2.5 times that of Lyu et al. at log L_bol = 44.5 (76 d against 30 d; 3.5 times at 43.5 and 1.6 times at 45.5), and the fitted lags are 0.54 of it in the median (a comparison added after the plan, without a null distribution). No published direct measurement of the W1-to-W2 lag was found in this search, so this does not show what else is mixed into the fitted lag.

*Stage 10: the optical light curve as the driver.* (Criteria in [docs/PREDICTIONS_10.md](docs/PREDICTIONS_10.md), committed before the ZTF light curves of this stage were downloaded or opened; written with the stage 9 results known. Type 1 objects only: 234 in the two samples, 135 pass the data criteria with 8–9 usable WISE visits each, and 32 are detected in both bands against swapped drivers. Statistics are for those 32.)

- *Sign.* With W1 and W2 each fitted to the ZTF light curve through a top-hat kernel, W2 again lags W1: τ2 − τ1 > 0 in 81% of the objects, with a median of 25 d (observed frame). Object by object it is rank-correlated with the stage 9 lag (Spearman ρ = +0.47, p = 0.007).
- *Lags behind the optical.* Medians of 110 d (W1) and 157.5 d (W2) in the observed frame. For the 24 objects with a bolometric luminosity they are 0.57 and 0.50 of the relation of Mandal et al. (2024), shorter in every variant tried (resampling of the objects up to 0.74 and 0.71; their equations before the disc correction 0.63 and 0.57; log L shifted by −0.3 dex 0.75 and 0.63). Against Lyu, Rieke & Smith (2019) the ratios are 0.74 and 0.69, but 1.02 and 0.94 when log L is shifted by −0.3 dex: that difference depends on the luminosity scale, and the definition of the BASS luminosity was not confirmed in the paper. Labels by the committed rule: "differs" for both relations. These are centres of the best-fitting top-hat, which come out 58–97 d shorter than the mean lag when the true kernel has a long tail.
- *What moves with the processing.* The median τ2 − τ1 is 0.63 of the stage 9 median for the same objects (label "differs" by the rule), but the resampling interval of that ratio is 0.25–1.44, and the median moves between 12.5 and 50 d with the kernel shape, the r band, linear interpolation of the driver or the removal of objects flagged for seeing. A term without delay improves the fits (median share 0.16 in W1; label "an undelayed component is present"), but the share is the same in W2 (0.15), a broad kernel gives the same signature on synthetic samples, and it drops to 0.05 in the 17 objects without a seeing flag (56 of the 135 objects have optical residuals that correlate with seeing). It is not identified as accretion-disc light, and it does not account for the "1.4 times" of stage 9: with the optical driver the lag difference is 0.33 of the Lyu difference (resampling interval 0.19–1.25), so there was nothing longer to explain (label "undetermined").
- *Re-formation.* On synthetic samples a fixed kernel and a response whose inner edge follows the luminosity history were not separated (power 0), so the test was not tried on the real light curves. The statement stays at: **consistent with an inter-band-lag model; not distinguished from re-formation.**
- *Limit of the labels.* The synthetic null samples are cleaner than the data: χ² per degree of freedom 0.5–0.9 against 1.8 in the real fits, and a detected fraction of 0.47–0.73 against 0.24. Labels made from those null ranges say more than the data decide, which is why the resampling intervals (post hoc) are given next to them.

**3. CGCG 312-012 in X-rays.** (Stage 7, registered in [docs/XRAY_PLAN.md](docs/XRAY_PLAN.md) before the data were downloaded; "7b" is a post hoc supplement written after the results and an outside review, section 9 of the same file.)

- *Observed.* On 2022-05-01 the Swift XRT 2–10 keV count rate was 3.3 ± 0.6 times that of 2010-12-29 (6.5σ). The NuSTAR 14–50 keV flux of the same day was 3.4 times the Swift-BAT 2004–2017 average in the same band (+0.53 dex, 90% interval +0.44 to +0.63, BAT channels 1–4 only).
- *Model-dependent.* With an absorbed power law (M0, the model picked by the registered rule) the intrinsic 2–10 keV luminosity rose by a factor of 4.1 (2.7–6.2) if the fitted XRT/NuSTAR normalisation of 0.68 applies to both XRT observations, or 6.0 (4.1–8.8) if it applies only to 2022; the two fits have the same statistic and cannot be told apart. With a reflection component and an iron line (M2, AIC lower by 36.7; the registered sequential rule stopped before testing it) the direct continuum of 2010 is not constrained. A change of the absorbing column is undecided (+0.13 dex, −0.06 to +0.35).
- *Interpretation.* The X-ray source was in a high state 33 days after the brightest WISE visit; when the infrared flare actually peaked between the six-month visits is not known. This is what a brightening of the AGN itself would give, and the registered verdict was "brightening of the AGN supported; a change of obscuration not excluded". It remains a **candidate** dust echo. The factor by which the intrinsic luminosity rose and the state of the source in 2010 are not determined by these data; that is the final result of stage 7. The dust luminosity at the brightest visit is about 4% of a bolometric luminosity estimated from the fitted 2–10 keV luminosity with an assumed correction of 20: an energy requirement, not a measurement of the covering factor.

## What was done

1. **Pipeline and known-answer test.** WISE and NEOWISE single-exposure photometry (2010–2024, W1 3.4 μm and W2 4.6 μm) is binned into visits about six months apart. A per-visit zero point from 10 comparison stars per field (differential photometry) and an error floor for extended galaxies, measured against the unTimely catalogue, are applied. The pipeline was run on NGC 3786, whose 2020 flare was reported by Son et al. (2022).
2. **Detection limits.** Injection–recovery on 2,310 comparison stars (about 1.1 million trials).
3. **Two frozen samples.** `sample_v1`: 231 Swift-BAT AGN at z ≤ 0.040 (225 enter the statistics). `sample_v2`: 210 AGN at 0.040 < z ≤ 0.30, with no object in common. For each sample the light curves were downloaded, quality-checked without any target variability statistic, frozen and hashed (`data/freeze_v1.json`, `data/freeze_v2.json`); predictions and decision rules were committed; estimators were validated on toy models; only then were the frozen files opened.
4. **Analyses.** Descriptive predictions Q1–Q3, a "dust breathing" hypothesis against Eddington ratio, a hysteresis statistic D, a test of an inter-band-lag model against luminosity in the second sample, and a classification of four infrared flare candidates against criteria committed beforehand.
5. **X-ray follow-up of one flare candidate.** Archival NuSTAR and Swift XRT observations of CGCG 312-012 from 2022, a Swift XRT observation from 2010 and the Swift-BAT 157-month average spectrum were reprocessed with HEASoft and fitted with XSPEC, against predictions and decision rules committed before the download; a post hoc supplement followed an outside review.
6. **Validation of the hysteresis statistic.** A null test on loop-free light curves, a direct comparison of the two branches that shares none of the steps of D, and a re-test of the lag model with a driver that depends on mass and luminosity.
7. **Transfer between the bands.** A test of whether a fixed kernel from W1 to W2 gives the measured size of the asymmetry: identifiability on synthetic samples first, then the fit to the frozen light curves, verdicts against synthetic samples that carry the fitted lags, and a comparison of the lags with published lag–luminosity relations.
8. **Optical driver.** For the type 1 objects, ZTF g or r light curves binned to nights, a damped-random-walk interpolation across the seasonal gaps, and a separate top-hat kernel from the optical light curve to W1 and to W2: identifiability on synthetic samples first, then the fit, a detection test against the light curves of other objects, and sensitivity checks. The analysis stops here.

## Results

**Known-answer test (NGC 3786).** W1 brightened by 0.560 mag and W2 by 0.855 mag (Son et al. 2022: about 0.5 and 0.8 mag), with the onset between the 2020-05 and 2020-12 visits (published: mid-2020) and W1−W2 going from 0.21 to 0.50. A second photometry (unTimely) and an independent script that does not use the pipeline code agree; the flux excess agrees to 1.2% in W1 and 6.9% in W2.

**Injection–recovery.** Rises of 0.3 mag or more were recovered in essentially all trials; a 0.2 mag rise in 76–100% depending on rise time.

**What this does and does not show.** The colour asymmetry appears in two disjoint samples, is not produced by the estimator on loop-free data, and is seen without the reference visits, the shared peak temperature and the blackbody that D uses. Its size is what a fixed linear response from W1 to W2 with a lag of 40–50 d gives, and nothing beyond such a response was found. The cause stays open: slow re-formation of dust was the motivating idea, and with six-month visits it cannot be told apart from a fixed lag between the bands, from a broader W2 response or from undelayed disc light in W1. With an optical driver, for the type 1 objects in which the infrared response is detected, W2 again follows W1 and both bands follow the optical with lags shorter than the relation of Mandal et al. (2024). Not shown: that there is no re-formation, that the lag comes from the geometry of the dust, what the width of the kernel is, whether the lag difference of stage 10 differs from that of stage 9, or that W1 contains undelayed disc light.

**Infrared flare candidates (side result).** Four objects flagged by the sudden-flare detector were classified against pre-committed criteria. Two are consistent with an AGN flare and are known objects (2MASX J12005792+0648226, in the MIRONG list of Jiang et al. 2021; NGC 5273, a known changing-look AGN). For NGC 513 an instrumental artefact is not excluded. CGCG 312-012 (Seyfert 2) is a mid-infrared nuclear flare **candidate**: W1 +0.50 mag and W2 +0.82 mag at a peak near 2022.2, no optical change in ZTF or ASAS-SN at about 0.05–0.10 mag (3σ). **No report of it was found in this search**, which covered a SIMBAD reference list, two flare catalogues and a web search, but not TNS or ADS. Infrared photometry alone cannot separate a tidal disruption inside an AGN from AGN variability. The X-ray analysis of this object is summarised in layer 3 above; no paper analysing the 2022 NuSTAR and Swift observations was found in this search (titles of the SIMBAD reference list and web searches, not ADS).

## Related work and how this statistic differs

The closest sample-level study is Son, Kim & Ho (2026, A&A 706, A122), from the group whose NGC 3786 paper this project started from. They compare ensemble structure functions computed from pairs of epochs in which the later one is brighter with those from pairs in which it is fainter, in ZTF g and NEOWISE W1 and W2, for optically selected AGN at 0.15 < z < 0.4 (7,443 type 1 and 6,855 type 2). For the samples as a whole they find the mid-infrared variability to be symmetric in time. When the type 1 objects are divided by optical-to-mid-infrared colour, those with bluer g − W1 show the larger amplitude when brightening and those with redder g − W1 when dimming, most clearly on time scales above a few hundred days; the g band shows no comparable asymmetry, and the dependence is absent or much weaker when the division is made by W1 − W2. From composite light curves they attribute the asymmetry to gradual global brightening and dimming trends, and discuss it as a sign that the amount of hot dust regulates itself. (This summary follows the arXiv PDF, 2601.00358v1, read on 2026-11-19. The earlier version of this paragraph was written from the abstract and left out that the dependence is on g − W1 colour and that the authors explain it by global trends.)

The quantity measured here is different. It is a difference of W1 − W2 *colour* between the brightening and the fading branch of one object at the same W2 flux, not a difference of variability *amplitude* across an ensemble. A global trend in brightness, which drives their result, does not by itself produce a branch offset in colour: with a linear term in time added to the direct comparison the brightening branch is still bluer in 179 of 211 and 189 of 200 objects. The sample is selected in hard X-rays at lower redshift and includes obscured nuclei, and the result is tested against a specified alternative, a fixed response between the two bands (stage 9; the stage 6B test against a lag–luminosity model was found in stage 8 to depend on the assumed scatter of that relation). Whether the two asymmetries come from the same process in the torus is an open question.

Other related work: Wu et al. (2026, arXiv:2611.04236) report a loop in the mid-infrared colour–magnitude diagram of one recurrent changing-look Seyfert, SDSS J234728.73+242745.7, read as the delayed response of an extended torus. Izviekova, Kompaniiets & Vavilova (2026, arXiv:2607.17815) find phase-resolved W1−W2 hysteresis in a mid-infrared-selected TDE candidate, UGC 11487, with W2 delayed by 224 ± 45 d and broader; this supports the delayed, broader W2 reading for a single transient. Wu et al. (2026, arXiv:2609.12390) study W1−W2 against W1 for 6,090 Seyfert 1s (bluer- and redder-when-brighter sources, host dilution) without separating branches. Within the literature checked, no study combines a hard-X-ray-selected sample, a same-flux comparison of rising and fading branches and a test of a specified lag model; the check used web searches and abstracts, not a systematic ADS query, so this is not a claim of priority.

## Reproducing

Requirements: Python ≥ 3.11 and the packages in `requirements.txt` (`pip install -r requirements.txt`, or `pip install -e ".[dev]"`).

```bash
python -m pytest                               # unit tests; tests that need the data cache or frozen files are skipped
python scripts/restore_catalog_columns.py      # download BAT 105-month and BASS tables, fill the blank catalogue columns
python scripts/fetch_data.py                   # stage 1-4 fields (NGC 3786 and controls) from IRSA
python scripts/run_analysis.py                 # known-answer test
python scripts/run_stage34.py                  # detectors, injection, physics, unTimely comparison
python scripts/fetch_sample.py                 # sample_v1 fields from IRSA (about 2 hours); IRECHO_SAMPLE=v2 for sample_v2
python scripts/fetch_untimely.py --sample
python scripts/run_qa.py                       # blind quality checks, error floors, frozen light curves
python scripts/run_stage5b.py                  # Q1-Q3, dust breathing, hysteresis (sample_v1)
python scripts/run_stage6b.py                  # hysteresis and luminosity (sample_v2); run with IRECHO_SAMPLE=v2
python scripts/run_stage8_noise.py             # stage 8: W1-W2 error correlation (needs the cache); again with IRECHO_SAMPLE=v2
python scripts/run_stage8.py null 2            # stage 8: null test (about 50 minutes with 2 workers)
python scripts/run_stage8.py direct            # stage 8: direct comparison
python scripts/run_stage8.py lag 2             # stage 8: lag model re-test (about 25 minutes)
python scripts/run_stage8.py select; python scripts/plot_stage8.py
python scripts/run_stage9.py ident 2           # stage 9: identifiability on synthetic samples (about 65 minutes with 2 workers)
python scripts/run_stage9.py fit               # stage 9: transfer W1 -> W2 on the frozen light curves
python scripts/run_stage9.py null 2            # stage 9: synthetic samples with the fitted lags (about 6 minutes)
python scripts/run_stage9.py verdict; python scripts/run_stage9.py mock5 2; python scripts/plot_stage9.py
python scripts/fetch_ztf10.py objects          # stage 10: which type 1 positions have a ZTF object (after restore_catalog_columns.py)
python scripts/fetch_ztf10.py curves --workers 3; python scripts/fetch_ztf10.py meta   # ZTF light curves (about 2 hours) and seeing
python scripts/run_stage10.py build            # stage 10: nightly light curves, criteria, flags, freeze
python scripts/run_stage10.py ident --workers 3; python scripts/run_stage10.py ident-summary   # synthetic samples (about 37 minutes)
python scripts/run_stage10.py fit              # stage 10: optical driver -> W1, W2 on the real light curves, swapped drivers, time reversal
python scripts/run_stage10.py fitted --workers 3; python scripts/run_stage10.py fitted-summary   # null samples with the fitted lags
python scripts/stage10_verdict.py; python scripts/run_stage10.py checks; python scripts/plot_stage10.py
python scripts/run_stage7.py                   # X-ray verdict table from the committed fit results (after restore_catalog_columns.py)
python scripts/run_stage7b.py                  # X-ray supplement: table and figure from the committed results
```

The X-ray fits themselves need HEASoft 6.37.1 (installed with conda into a git-ignored `.heasoft/` folder), the calibration files and the archive data (`scripts/fetch_xray.py`, 322 MB; `scripts/fetch_caldb_subset.py`); the steps are `scripts/xray_process.sh`, `scripts/xray_fit.py` and `scripts/xray_7b.py`, listed in docs/PIPELINE.md sections 15–17. The final fits are saved as XSPEC command files and logs in `results/s7b_xcm/`. None of the X-ray event files, spectra, response matrices, calibration files or the HEASoft environment is in this repository; `results/s7_spectra_plot.json` holds only the binned points drawn in the spectrum figure.

The exact order, options and run times are in [docs/PIPELINE.md](docs/PIPELINE.md). Raw IRSA downloads go to `data/cache/` and frozen light curves to `data/frozen/`; both are git-ignored and are **not** in this repository. `data/freeze_v1.json` and `data/freeze_v2.json` hold the SHA-256 of every frozen light curve, and the analysis scripts stop if a rebuilt file does not match; `data/freeze_v3.json` does the same for the ZTF files of stage 10 (raw files and nightly light curves). The stage 10 commands were run in the private working copy only; in a fresh clone of this repository they were not run end to end. IRSA can reprocess or re-serve tables, so a fresh download is not guaranteed to reproduce the hashes; I have not tested a full rebuild on a second machine.

Tests: 323 pass in this repository as published and 61 are skipped (regression tests that need the local IRSA cache and the frozen light curves, and one that needs a catalogue value); with those present the suite runs all 384. The toy model in `sim/` has its own 9 tests (`cd sim && node --test model.test.mjs`).

## Limits

- Six-month sampling cannot measure an inter-band lag directly. A stacked W1–W2 cross-correlation estimator failed its validation on simulated data and was not applied. The direct comparison shows that the colours of the two branches differ; it does not say why.
- The stage 9 lags come from a linear interpolation of W1 between visits. In the synthetic tests the sample median is recovered within 10 d, but the error for a single object has an rms of 21–68 d, and the interpolation alone leaves a residual of −1.6 to −5.9 mmag for true lags of 20–40 d. With no lag in the truth, 1.5–4.9% of the objects are still flagged "well determined". A fixed lag, a kernel of different width, re-formation and undelayed light in W1 are not separated by these data.
- The synthetic samples of stage 9 are rougher than the real light curves (see the note under the stage 9 table); the strength of the driver on time scales shorter than the visit spacing cannot be set from these data. The stage 9 criteria were written after the results up to stage 8 had been seen. The fit was not repeated with the unTimely photometry or with another interpolation.
- Lag against luminosity: the equations of Lyu, Rieke & Smith (2019) were read from the arXiv v2 PDF (page 17); Table 5 of Mandal et al. (2024) was read through a summarising tool from the ar5iv text, not from the PDF. Both give lags of each band behind the optical; their difference is used here. The BASS bolometric luminosity is not the luminosity defined in those papers, and no correction was made. Only objects with a luminosity enter (347 of 435).
- Stage 10 covers type 1 objects only, and its statistics rest on the 32 objects (24 with a luminosity) in which both bands are detected; with 8–9 usable visits two slowly varying curves often match by chance (the 95th percentile of the fit correlation with a swapped driver is 0.95). Objects whose lag sits at the end of the grid (465 d) are not counted as detected, so long lags of luminous objects may be cut.
- Stage 10 lags are centres of the best-fitting top-hat on a driver interpolated across seasonal gaps (44–49% of the kernel window lies in optical gaps longer than 30 d). The optical light curves are PSF photometry: in 56 of 135 objects the residuals correlate with seeing, which was flagged and not corrected. The lag difference is not stable between the separate and the joint fit of the two bands (9 of 32 objects outside the joint 95% interval).
- The synthetic null samples of stage 10 are cleaner than the data (see layer 2), the resampling intervals were added after the results, and the BASS bolometric luminosity was not converted to the 5100 Å-based luminosities of the two published relations. The equations of both papers were read from the arXiv PDFs for this stage.
- The variable component is treated as a single-temperature blackbody; host light is assumed constant; in type 1 AGN the W1 flux can include accretion-disc light.
- The baseline is the mean of the three faintest visits, which is not a true floor for objects that never faded in ten years.
- The "measurable" cut selects objects with large variability, and the selection 210 → 94 → 70 (measurable, with a bolometric luminosity) was not reproduced in the simulations.
- Eddington ratio, type, absorbing column and luminosity are correlated in the sample; stacked trends cannot be assigned to one of them. Catalogue values were used as published, without their uncertainties.
- Differences computed after the results were seen (for example a higher median D in the brighter half) are labelled post hoc in the result files and are not part of any verdict.
- Literature checks used web searches and abstracts; ADS was not queried systematically.
- The pre-registration protects against tuning on the target data. It does not make the estimator unbiased. The null test of stage 8 covers two kinds of loop-free light curves and starts from the visit level, not from single exposures; the error correlation it uses mixes the errors of two photometries.
- The stage 8 criteria were written after the results of stages 5b and 6B had been seen; the document lists what was already known.
- Lag-model re-test: the simulated samples have about half the measurable objects of the real one and a larger D, and the variability relations of MacLeod et al. (2010), fitted to SDSS Stripe 82 quasars, are applied to less luminous Seyferts.
- X-rays: the XRT/NuSTAR normalisation of 0.65–0.68 is unexplained (the good-time intervals of the two instruments overlap for 62 s of 6,963 s, so variability between them is not excluded). The synthetic-spectrum validation only recovered model M0 from M0, and its 90% interval for the photon index covered the truth in 85.5% of 200 trials (2.1σ below 90%). The cross-calibration terms of 0.06 and 0.04 dex are assumptions whose basis was not confirmed in the literature. Everything labelled 7b was done after the registered results were seen.
